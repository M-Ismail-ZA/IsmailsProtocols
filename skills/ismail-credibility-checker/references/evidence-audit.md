# Epistemic Protocol: Evidence Audit

This is the sourcing pass behind the 8-rule "Epistemic Protocol for Evaluating Novel Claims, Theories, or Technologies," done ahead of turning it into a Claude skill. For each rule it separates three things: what real research or documented history actually backs it, what complicates it, and a verdict. Rule 1 asks for zero epistemic skew, so the fairest way to treat the protocol is to hold it to that same standard rather than only hunting for citations that flatter it. Two things turned up worth flagging before the rule-by-rule material: several of the strongest "supporting" papers have their own published critics, and Rule 5 turns out to be in real tension with Rules 3, 6, and 7 once you look at how formal verification is actually used. Both get their own section below rather than getting smoothed over.

Every citation was checked against a retrievable source rather than pulled from memory and left unverified. Where I couldn't verify something to my own satisfaction, I've said so rather than including it anyway.

**A note on numbering.** This document keeps the original rule numbers and order from the source document that started this project—Rule 3 here is the hierarchy of verification, Rule 5 is the definitional audit, and so on, exactly as first presented. ## Rule 1: Assume the Null Position

**The claim:** treat a new proposition neutrally, resisting both the halo effect (favoring it because it's polished, prestigious, or agreeable) and its mirror image (dismissing it for being unfamiliar or from an outsider).

**Supporting evidence.** The halo effect itself is one of the oldest, most-replicated findings in applied psychology. Edward Thorndike's 1920 paper ["A Constant Error in Psychological Ratings"](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/4_J_Applied_Psychology_25_(Thorndike).pdf) found that when Army officers rated soldiers on traits meant to be independent—physique, intelligence, leadership, character—the ratings correlated far too strongly with each other to reflect real independence. A single overall impression was driving every specific judgment. That's the positive-skew half of Rule 1. The negative-skew half—rejecting something for being unfamiliar or unaffiliated—is covered by the much broader literature on confirmation bias, most comprehensively surveyed in Raymond Nickerson's 1998 review ["Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175), which documents the tendency to seek and weigh evidence in ways partial to a belief already held, across dozens of experimental paradigms.

A commonly cited "outsider-rejection" study is worth a specific caveat. Katz and Allen's 1982 paper on the [Not-Invented-Here syndrome](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9310.1982.tb00478.x) gets invoked constantly as evidence that groups reject good ideas simply because they came from outside. But its own abstract describes something narrower: a study of 50 R&D project groups, where performance declined with long project tenure, tracking a measurable drop in communication with people outside the group. The "self-sufficiency" framing that usually gets attached to it is a broader claim than tenure-and-communication patterns alone establish. It's a real phenomenon worth knowing about, just cited more confidently than the original data supports, which is itself a small, on-the-nose example of Rule 4.

**A real-world instance of the mechanism, not just the theory.** In 1936, Einstein and Nathan Rosen submitted a paper to *Physical Review* arguing that gravitational waves could not exist. The editor, following standard practice, sent it to a referee—later identified as Howard P. Robertson—who found a real problem in the argument. Einstein, apparently encountering an anonymous referee at that journal for the first time, dismissed the review as mistaken, objected to his manuscript being shown to an outside critic at all, and withdrew the paper rather than revise it. He then worked through the same objection informally with Robertson, corrected the argument, and republished it with the opposite conclusion in the *Journal of the Franklin Institute*—the corrected paper is what's now known as the Einstein–Rosen metric. Daniel Kennefick's account of the episode, ["Einstein Versus the *Physical Review*"](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/einstein-versus-the-physical-review) (Physics Today, 2005), is explicit that the referee's criticism turned out to be correct. It's a useful case precisely because the positive-skew failure here wasn't naivety—it was one of the most capable physicists in history dismissing a criticism on the grounds of who was making it.

**Verdict:** well-supported for both halves of the rule, with one citation (Katz & Allen) that needs a narrower reading than it usually gets.

## Rule 2: Separate the Medium from the Message

**The claim:** the clarity or density of how a claim is communicated says nothing about whether it's true. Jargon isn't depth; plain language isn't shallowness.

**Supporting evidence.** The cleanest direct test of the first half is Kimmo Eriksson's 2012 study, ["The Nonsense Math Effect"](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-nonsense-math-effect-Eriksson/13c3720739e4aa52191e962c363ee58cb2c1073b) (*Judgment and Decision Making*). Two hundred participants, all holding a postgraduate degree, read abstracts of real papers; some abstracts had an extra sentence bolted on containing a mathematical formula that was, in context, meaningless. Participants with degrees in the humanities, social sciences, or medicine rated the doctored abstracts as higher-quality research; participants with math or science training weren't fooled (the effect on this group was essentially at chance, 46%). The presence of math moved perceived rigor for exactly the audience least equipped to check it. On the second half—that accessible language isn't automatically more trustworthy—the relevant work is Pennycook, Cheyne, Barr, Koehler, and Fugelsang's 2015 paper on ["pseudo-profound bullshit"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/on-the-reception-and-detection-of-pseudoprofound-bullshit/0D3C87BCC238BCA38BC55E395BDC9999): syntactically normal, plain-English sentences generated by randomly combining vague buzzwords were rated as at least somewhat profound by most participants, and that susceptibility correlated with weaker performance on standard analytic-reasoning tests. (The paper's memorable premise earned it the 2016 Ig Nobel Peace Prize.) Between the two studies, both directions of Rule 2 have direct experimental support: complexity inflates perceived credibility for non-experts, and plainness inflates perceived meaning independent of whether there's any real content behind it.

**A scope clarification, more than a caveat.** The practical method here isn't "feed a dense document to an LLM and trust a full translation"—it's the same move Rule 5 already makes for formal proofs: check the definitions and the foundational framing, not everything built on top of them. That's a categorically smaller task, closer to reading an introduction and a setup section than to translating a hundred-page derivation. If the foundational definitions are sound and actually map onto the domain being claimed about, the derivation built on them can be checked by other means (replication, formal tools, a domain specialist) or provisionally trusted; if the definitions are unsound, nothing built on top of them matters, and there was never a need to read past the setup. Scoped this way, most of the hallucination risk drops out on its own—a few pages of definitions is something a person can spot-check line by line against the original; a long derivation isn't.

The residual risk is smaller, not zero. A short passage can still be subtly mistranslated, and the failure mode is well documented: research on LLM hallucination in technical and mathematical reasoning is now a substantial, active field (the 2025 ACM survey by Huang et al. catalogs the taxonomy), and outside math entirely, a 2023 New York court sanctioned attorneys after a brief they filed cited court cases invented by ChatGPT and never checked against a source. The lesson isn't "don't use the tool," it's "check the translated definition against the original wording for anything load-bearing"—cheap to do, given how short that passage now is.

**Verdict:** well-supported on both halves as stated, and the practical method holds up better once it's read as checking definitions and framing rather than translating an entire document—narrower scope, smaller risk, same principle.

## Rule 3: Establish the Epistemic Hierarchy of Verification

**The claim:** evidence sits on a ladder—anecdote, then peer-reviewed publication, then empirical reproduction, then machine-checked formal proof—and a claim at a higher rung can't be defeated by an objection from a lower one.

**Supporting evidence for the low end of the ladder.** The case that peer review alone is a weak filter is well documented, at least for empirical fields. The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 project, ["Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aac4716) (*Science*), directly replicated 100 studies from three major psychology journals: 97% of the original studies had reported statistically significant results, but only 36% of the replications did, and replication effect sizes averaged about half the original size. John Ioannidis's 2005 essay ["Why Most Published Research Findings Are False"](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.0020124) (*PLoS Medicine*) makes the theoretical case for why this happens—small studies, small effects, flexible analysis choices, and competitive fields all push published findings toward being wrong more often than intuition suggests. Worth noting in the spirit of Rule 1: Ioannidis's own paper has a serious published critic. Statisticians Steven Goodman and Sander Greenland argued in a [2007 response](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.0040168) in the same journal that the mathematical model underlying the "most findings are false" claim has a circularity problem and that Ioannidis's framework may itself overstate the crisis. The broad point (peer review is a plausibility filter, not a proof) survives; the specific numbers in the paper that popularized it are contested by working statisticians.

**The missing rung: consilience.** The original ladder skips a real category of evidence, and it belongs exactly where it was flagged as missing: past a single reproduced result, short of deductive proof. The term is old. William Whewell coined "consilience of inductions" in *The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences* (1840) for the specific case where an explanation built to account for one class of facts turns out, unprompted, to also account for a completely different class it wasn't built to explain. E.O. Wilson revived the term in his 1998 book *Consilience: The Unity of Knowledge*, extending it—more controversially than Whewell's original, narrower claim—into an argument for unifying the natural sciences with the humanities.

The cleanest textbook case is Jean Perrin's argument for the physical reality of atoms. Between 1908 and 1913, [Perrin obtained Avogadro's number](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/16017/1/Coko-Jean%20Perrin%20and%20the%20Philosophers'%20Stories.pdf)—the number of molecules in a mole of a substance—through thirteen independent experimental methods: Brownian motion, radioactive decay, viscosity, diffusion, blackbody radiation, and others, each resting on entirely different physics. All thirteen converged on close to the same value. That convergence, not any single measurement, is what ended a decades-long dispute over whether atoms were physically real or just a useful bookkeeping fiction, and it's substantially why Perrin won the 1926 Nobel Prize in Physics. (Philosophers of science draw a precise distinction worth knowing here: Perrin's argument—independent methods converging on the same *number*—is technically "robustness," while Whewell reserved "consilience" for a theory explaining a different *kind* of fact than the one it was built for. The two get used interchangeably in practice, including by Wilson, but they're not quite the same claim.)

**Supporting evidence for the high end of the ladder.** Formal machine-checked proof really has resolved disputes that community trust alone couldn't. The 1976 Appel–Haken proof of the Four Color Theorem relied on a computer checking an enormous number of cases that no human could verify by hand, and it was controversial for exactly that reason for nearly thirty years; Georges Gonthier's 2005 Coq formalization, described in [Gonthier (2008)](https://www.ams.org/notices/200811/tx081101382p.pdf) (*Notices of the AMS*), gave a fully machine-checked version that settled the lingering unease. The Kepler conjecture is an even sharper case: Thomas Hales's 1998 proof was reviewed by a panel of twelve mathematicians who spent over four years on it and could only report being "99% certain" it was correct, because parts of the argument depended on custom computer code they couldn't fully check by hand. That level of confidence wasn't good enough for Hales, so he spent the next decade-plus on the Flyspeck project, producing a complete, machine-checked proof in 2014 (official account in [Hales et al., *A Formal Proof of the Kepler Conjecture*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.02155), *Forum of Mathematics, Pi*, 2017). Hales later wrote his own candid [critical retrospective](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.08032) on the whole project, which is worth reading precisely because it doesn't oversell what formalization achieved.

**Telling real consilience from numerology.** A consilience argument gets all its strength from the domains being genuinely independent and the match being structural, not just numerical—which is exactly where it can be faked, and the fake version deserves naming precisely because it wears the same clothes as the real thing. In 1994, *Statistical Science* published a paper by Doron Witztum, Eliyahu Rips, and Yoav Rosenberg claiming that letters spaced at regular intervals through the Hebrew text of Genesis encoded the names and dates of rabbis born centuries after the text was written, verified to a p-value of 0.000016. A [1999 rebuttal in the same journal](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/statistical-science/volume-14/issue-2/Solving-the-Bible-Code-Puzzle/10.1214/ss/1009212243.full), by Brendan McKay, Dror Bar-Natan, Maya Bar-Hillel, and Gil Kalai, ran the identical method on *Moby-Dick* and *War and Peace* and turned up comparably "significant" assassination predictions in both. The method had enough flexibility—which spelling of a name, which date format, how "close together" counts—that it could find a plausible match in any sufficiently long text, and no one had checked that until someone tried a text with no business containing it.

That move—run the same method on a case it has no reason to work on—is the actual test for a spurious numerical resemblance like a random timestamp dividing out close to the golden ratio. [George Markowsky's widely-cited 1992 critique](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07468342.1992.11973428) of golden-ratio claims in art and architecture (*The College Mathematics Journal*) found the same pattern behind most of them: claims about the Parthenon or the Great Pyramid typically rest on measuring between whichever points give something close to 1.618, out of many available points, with a generous tolerance applied only after the fact. Compare a genuine case: the golden ratio's real appearance in phyllotaxis, the spiral seed pattern in a sunflower head. There, a proven mechanism—the golden angle (about 137.5°) is mathematically the rotation that minimizes overlap between successive seeds—explains why that specific number and no other shows up, and the same mechanism correctly predicts it will recur in unrelated plants, which it does. [Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory of analogy](https://philpapers.org/rec/GENSAT-2) (*Cognitive Science*, 1983) gives this the precise name it deserves: a genuine cross-domain match requires shared *relational* structure—the same system of causal or logical connections—not a shared surface number. A pattern that survives only because a numerator, denominator, and tolerance were chosen after seeing the answer has a coincidence and a story bolted on afterward, not a structure.

One caution in the other direction, so this doesn't overclaim either: Larry Laudan's 1981 paper ["A Confutation of Convergent Realism"](https://philosophy.hku.hk/courses/dm/phil2130/AConfutationOfConvergentRealism2_Laudan.pdf) (*Philosophy of Science*) catalogs a list of past theories—phlogiston, the luminiferous ether among them—that had genuine, multi-line empirical support by the standards of their time and were still eventually replaced. It's a contested argument (Stathis Psillos and others have pushed back on how many of Laudan's examples really had that kind of broad, independent support), but the caution is worth keeping regardless: consilience raises confidence enormously. It doesn't retire the possibility of error the way a machine-checked proof retires the possibility of a logical mistake.

**Why this is a domain-relative spectrum, not one strict ladder.** The rule's own wording—"they cannot defeat a Level 4 claim with a Level 2 response"—states a strict override chain, and taken that literally, it overreaches twice over: once by ignoring that different domains have different achievable ceilings, and once by collapsing two different kinds of residual work into a single axis.

On the ceiling question: formal proof and consilience aren't really rungs on the same ladder so much as the top of two different ladders, one for each kind of claim. A mathematical or logical claim can, in principle, be pushed all the way to a machine-checked proof, because it's a deductive claim about what follows from stated axioms. An empirical claim about the world—what a psychological mechanism does, whether a compound is safe, how a species evolved—can never get that kind of proof, because it isn't a deductive claim in the first place; the strongest evidence achievable for it is exactly the Perrin move, independent unrelated methods converging. Demanding Level-4-style proof from a psychological theory isn't holding it to a higher standard, it's asking for a category of evidence that doesn't exist for that category of claim.

The mismatch runs the other way too, and this is the direction most easily missed: a purely deductive claim needs no empirical confirmation at all to be accepted, because it isn't making a claim about the world in the first place. "There are infinitely many primes" doesn't require a single real-world observation; the proof is the whole of what there is to check, and asking what evidence "backs it up" out in reality is a category error, not a request for rigor. Confusion tends to creep in only once a formal result gets applied to something real—an algorithm proven correct in Lean is then asserted to solve an actual scheduling problem well, say. That application is a separate, additional empirical claim, sitting on its own rung of the inductive ladder and needing its own evidence; it doesn't retroactively require the original proof to acquire empirical support, and the proof's validity doesn't retroactively exempt the applied claim from scrutiny either. Treating the two ladders as though they must confirm each other—a theorem isn't fully trustworthy until something empirical backs it up, or strong consilience isn't fully trustworthy until someone formalizes it—is the same mistake as demanding proof from the psychological theory, just run in the opposite direction.

The reverse mismatch on the *empirical* side also happens: a narrow, single-study, mechanistically well-understood finding doesn't need multi-domain consilience to be credible, any more than it needs a Lean proof. That bar is appropriate for paradigm-level claims whose scope inherently spans several fields—evolution, atomic theory, plate tectonics—not for every domain-specific result that comes along.

Underneath that, the second collapse still holds from the earlier draft of this analysis: there are two different kinds of residual work an evaluator does within whichever ladder applies, and formal verification only shrinks one of them. The first is *internal-logical assurance*—how much work is left to check that the argument's steps actually follow. Here the hierarchy is real. Once a claim is machine-checked, that work drops to almost nothing: you're trusting a small, heavily-exercised proof kernel, not re-deriving a long argument by hand. A peer-reviewed finding leaves far more of that work undone—the OSC replication numbers above (36% replicating against 97% of originals reporting significance) are a direct measure of how much peer review does *not* settle. On this axis, a new psychological theory genuinely does need more evaluative effort than a new theorem checked in Lean; the rule is correct about that, flatly.

The second is *definitional mapping*—whether the checked statement is the statement you actually care about. Neither formal verification nor consilience shrinks this one; both can be faked or misapplied relative to it, which is exactly what the Bible Code affair and golden-ratio numerology above show for consilience, and what a mis-specified Lean theorem would show for formal proof. The clearest evidence for the formal-proof side is already in this document: the Liquid Tensor Experiment's Lean proof, completed in July 2022, is airtight given its formal statement—but getting that statement right took Johan Commelin's team a year and a half of expert human work *before any formal checking began*, precisely because Peter Scholze wanted independent confirmation that the statement matched what he actually meant. That's the same labor a claim needs whether the eventual proof takes a computer a millisecond or a mathematician a decade to check, and no amount of internal-logic assurance, or consilience, substitutes for it. Richard De Millo, Richard Lipton, and Alan Perlis made a version of this argument in their 1979 paper ["Social Processes and Proofs of Theorems and Programs"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/359104.359106) (*Communications of the ACM*)—that trust in mathematics comes from a community reading, checking, and using a proof over time, not from symbol-manipulation alone. It's a genuinely contested position (a public discussion of the paper in 2021, prompted partly by the [Liquid Tensor Experiment](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/lte-final/), included the view that parts of the 1979 argument "look, at best, dated" now that large-scale Lean formalization exists)—but the part of it that holds up isn't about whether formal proof is trustworthy, it's about who has to do the work of stating the theorem correctly, and that work stays social no matter how good the checker gets.

**Verdict:** the rule is right that peer review alone is a weak filter, and right, on the internal-logical-assurance axis, that a new psychological theory needs more evaluative effort than a new Lean theorem—that specific hierarchy is real and defensible. It's missing a genuine rung, consilience, documented since Whewell (1840) and demonstrated cleanly by Perrin's work on Avogadro's number, which functions as the ceiling for empirical claims the way formal proof is the ceiling for deductive ones—so the fix isn't inserting consilience as one more step below proof, it's recognizing that different claim types top out on different ladders entirely, and demanding the wrong ladder's ceiling from a claim is a category error in either direction. The fake version of that same rung is easy to manufacture (numerology, the Bible Code) and reasonably easy to catch, by checking for shared relational structure and testing the method on a case where it has no business working. None of that changes the earlier finding: whichever ladder applies, and however high a claim has climbed it, the definitional-mapping question is a separate axis that Rule 5 still has to answer on its own—see the cross-cutting section for a live, unresolved case where that's exactly what's being argued about.

## Rule 4: Recognize Social Signals as Marketing, Not Truth

**The claim:** citation counts, journal prestige, institutional affiliation, and how much a topic is being discussed are measures of attention, not accuracy.

**Supporting evidence.** Per Seglen's 1997 BMJ paper, ["Why the Impact Factor of Journals Should Not Be Used for Evaluating Research"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9056804/), citations within any given journal are extremely skewed—a small fraction of articles account for most of a journal's citations—so a journal-level average predicts almost nothing about any single article in it. That argument, plus others like it, is exactly why the [San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Declaration_on_Research_Assessment) (DORA), drafted in December 2012 at a meeting of the American Society for Cell Biology, explicitly recommends against using journal impact factor as a stand-in for the quality of an individual paper—a recommendation tens of thousands of researchers and institutions have since signed onto. The strongest empirical version of this argument is Fang and Casadevall's 2011 editorial introducing the ["retraction index"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3187237/): across the 17 journals they studied, retractions per article correlated positively with impact factor—the more prestigious journals in their sample didn't retract less, they retracted more (a finding the *New England Journal of Medicine*, which topped their list, publicly disputed on methodological grounds).

The clearest illustration that prestige and peer review don't guarantee correctness is the Jan Hendrik Schön case. Between 1998 and 2002, the Bell Labs physicist published dazzling results in organic electronics and superconductivity at a pace of roughly one paper every eight days, in *Nature*, *Science*, and *Physical Review*. An independent investigation found fabricated or falsified data in 16 of the 24 papers it examined; Schön was fired, journals retracted at least 15 of his papers (eight from *Science*, seven from *Nature*, with more retracted elsewhere), and the University of Konstanz revoked his PhD in 2004. None of that was caught by peer review at the time—it took outside physicists noticing that supposedly independent noise plots in different experiments looked identical. The same pattern shows up, more chronically, in the ["Nobel disease"](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/disappearing-pod/the-nobel-disease/) phenomenon: a widely reported and fairly well-documented tendency for a handful of Nobel laureates to later champion fringe claims well outside their field of expertise—Linus Pauling and megadose vitamin C, Kary Mullis and HIV/AIDS skepticism alongside astrology, Luc Montagnier and claims about DNA emitting detectable electromagnetic signals. A Nobel Prize certifies one specific past achievement; it doesn't extend to whatever the laureate says next.

**Verdict:** well-supported, across independent lines of evidence (bibliometrics, a formal institutional declaration, and dramatic individual cases).

## Rule 5: Audit the Definitions

**The claim:** a formal proof is only as good as the definitions it's built on. If those definitions don't map onto what you actually care about, the proof—however rigorous—doesn't tell you what you think it tells you.

**Supporting evidence.** This is the oldest and, on the evidence, the most solid idea in the whole document. Imre Lakatos's 1976 book *[Proofs and Refutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofs_and_Refutations)* built its entire argument around a single example: Euler's formula for polyhedra, V − E + F = 2. Every time someone produced an apparent counterexample—a hollow solid built from a cube inside a cube, or two tetrahedra joined at a single edge—mathematicians didn't just accept the formula as refuted. They went back and changed what counted as a "polyhedron" in the first place, so the theorem and its domain evolved together. Lakatos's point generalizes directly: a "proof" is never separable from the definitions it's stated in terms of, and getting those definitions right is not itself a task a formal system can perform.

Software engineering has its own, cleaner version of the same idea, usually attributed to Barry Boehm: *verification* asks "are we building the product right?" while *validation* asks "are we building the right product?" ([summary here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_verification_and_validation)). A machine-checked proof is a verification exercise—it establishes that a conclusion follows from stated axioms—and formal methods have nothing to say about validation: whether those axioms are the right formalization of what you actually meant. The seL4 project, which produced the first complete machine-checked functional-correctness proof of a general-purpose operating-system kernel, is unusually candid about exactly this limit in its own public documentation: the team states plainly that a formal proof establishes only that an implementation matches its stated specification, not that the specification captures everything a person actually wants—and separately lists the assumptions the proof still rests on (compiler, hardware, and boot-code correctness, plus, in principle, the soundness of the theorem prover itself, which they judge as very low risk given how small and heavily exercised its trusted core is). That's a team that built one of the most celebrated formal-verification results in computer science volunteering, in writing, that the hardest part of "does this proof mean what I think it means" sits outside what the proof itself can check.

**Verdict:** well-supported, arguably the strongest-grounded rule here—and, as the next section covers, this is exactly what limits how far Rules 3, 6, and 7 can be pushed.

## Rule 6: Institutions Do Not Confer Infallibility

**The claim:** credentials and institutional affiliation are a heuristic for training, not a guarantee against error.

**Supporting evidence.** The sharpest direct test of this is Peters and Ceci's 1982 study, ["Peer-Review Practices of Psychological Journals: The Fate of Published Articles, Submitted Again"](https://philpapers.org/rec/ADAAPE) (*Behavioral and Brain Sciences*). They took twelve already-published articles, all originally from prestigious university departments, replaced the author names and affiliations with fictitious low-status ones, made only cosmetic changes, and resubmitted them to the same non-blind, high-rejection-rate journals that had published them 18 to 32 months earlier. Only 3 of 38 editors and reviewers noticed the papers had already been published. Of the nine that went through a full, undetected second review, eight were rejected—mostly on the grounds of "serious methodological flaws," in work the same journals had already judged good enough to print. The content hadn't changed; the names and affiliations had. The Schön and Einstein–Robertson cases described under Rules 4 and 1 cut in complementary directions on this same point: Schön shows a prestigious institution and top journals all failing to catch fabrication for years, while the Einstein case shows an "anonymous," relatively unknown referee turning out to be right against the most famous physicist alive—institutional prestige predicted neither outcome.

**Verdict:** well-supported. The rule's closing line—that formal verification simply "wins" the moment it contradicts community consensus—inherits the same limit flagged under Rule 3: it's only true once Rule 5's audit has actually been done, since a community's objection is very often exactly a Rule 5 objection about whether the definitions match reality, not a claim that the formal logic contains an error.

## Rule 7: The Burden of Proof Lies with the Claimant, and Tools Shift It

**The claim:** whoever makes a claim has to support it, and modern tools (LLMs, verification engines) have given observers a real capacity to check claims that didn't exist before.

**Supporting evidence for the classical half.** The principle that the burden of proof sits with whoever is asserting something, not with whoever doubts it, is old and well established. Bertrand Russell's 1952 essay "Is There a God?" makes the case with the now-famous image of an unfalsifiable [teapot orbiting the sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell%27s_teapot) between Earth and Mars: the fact that you can't disprove it isn't a reason to believe it. Christopher Hitchens gave the idea its most quoted modern phrasing in 2007—"what can be asserted without evidence can also be dismissed without evidence"—though as [Hitchens's razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitchens's_razor)'s own history shows, the idea traces back at least to a Latin legal maxim about the burden of proof, so Hitchens popularized it rather than invented it. Worth flagging in the interest of not overselling even the parts of this document that are on solid ground: both of these specific formulations have real philosophical critics (Brian Garvey, Peter van Inwagen, and Alvin Plantinga have all published objections to the teapot analogy's reach, and philosopher Michael Antony has argued Hitchens's razor, applied consistently, cuts against some of the same conclusions it's usually used to support). The underlying burden-of-proof principle is sound; neither specific slogan is beyond dispute.

**Where the "tools shift it" half needs a sharper line, not a blanket caveat.** For one specific tool—machine-checked formal proof—this half of the rule is demonstrably true, and Rule 3's Kepler and Four Color examples are direct evidence of it: problems that were once uncheckable by any individual are now mechanically verifiable. The rule is also right that this generalizes further than formal proof alone: this document is itself an instance of the same shift. Assembling roughly thirty independently-checkable citations across eight rules, each one a link the reader can click and verify against the primary source, is exactly the kind of audit that would have taken a research library and weeks of a specialist's time a generation ago, and took a few hours of tool-assisted search here. That's a real, meaningful expansion of who can discharge an audit burden, not a hypothetical one—the existence of this document is evidence for the rule, not just an application of it.

The distinction worth drawing explicitly is between two different things "use an LLM to check a claim" can mean: asking a model a question and trusting whatever it produces from memory, versus using a model to drive search and retrieval and then checking the result against the source it points to. Hallucination research (a 2025 survey in *ACM Transactions on Information Systems* catalogs the taxonomy) and the clearest cautionary case on record—a 2023 New York court sanctioning attorneys whose brief cited court cases ChatGPT had invented and no one had checked—are both failures of the first kind: unverified output treated as if it were the evidence. Neither shows up the same way in the second kind, where the model's output is a pointer to something independently checkable and the checking happens against that source, not against the model's say-so. This document was built the second way. The rule should say so explicitly rather than treating "use an LLM" as one undifferentiated capability.

**Verdict:** the classical burden-of-proof principle is well-supported (with real, citable philosophical pushback on the specific slogans). The claim that tools now shift the burden of proof holds up well for formal verification and for LLM-orchestrated, source-checked retrieval—and much less well for LLM output trusted on its own—a distinction the rule should draw explicitly rather than bundling "use an LLM" into a single capability.

## Rule 8: Maintain Epistemic Agility

**The claim:** methods of validation keep improving, and treating today's standard practice (like peer review) as a timeless gold standard is institutional inertia, not rigor.

**Supporting evidence.** The best evidence for this rule is that peer review itself is a much younger and less consistent practice than it's usually assumed to be—which is exactly the kind of thing this rule says to watch for. Daniel Kennefick's research (recounted in ["Einstein Versus the *Physical Review*"](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/einstein-versus-the-physical-review) and a related [Physics Today piece](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/in-referees-we-trust)) found, from the journal's own archived logbooks, that Einstein's earlier and far more radical papers at *Physical Review*—including the famous Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen paper—were never sent to a referee at all; the field for the referee's name was simply left blank. His 1936 gravitational-waves paper appears to have been his first-ever encounter with the anonymous refereeing system now considered standard, and he reacted to it as an unwelcome novelty rather than an established norm, which by 1936 it essentially wasn't yet, even at a major American physics journal. That's direct evidence that "the way real science gets checked" has changed materially within living memory of methods this protocol treats as new (computer proof assistants, LLM-assisted review), and will likely keep changing.

**Verdict:** well-supported. This rule is more a methodological stance than a single testable claim, but the historical record backs the specific point it's making: current practice is contingent, not eternal.

## The cross-cutting problem: Rules 3, 5, 6, and 7 don't fully agree with each other

This is the most important finding of this audit, and it's about the structure of the protocol rather than any single citation.

Rules 3, 6, and 7, read on their own, add up to something like: *a formally verified claim sits at the top of the evidence hierarchy, institutions can't override it, and a challenge that isn't itself formalized doesn't meet it on its own level.* Rule 5 quietly cuts the legs out from under that. A formal proof only ever establishes that a conclusion follows *given a particular set of definitions*—and whether those definitions are a faithful rendering of what's actually being claimed about the world, or about a finance or mathematics problem, is a question a formal system cannot answer about itself. Checking that is precisely the kind of expert, social, communal judgment that Rules 3, 6, and 7 present as something a strong-enough formal result can bypass.

This isn't hypothetical. The Liquid Tensor Experiment—this document's best example *for* Rule 3—is simultaneously the best evidence *against* reading it too strongly. [Kevin Buzzard's account](https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2022/09/12/beyond-the-liquid-tensor-experiment/) of the project is direct that the formal machinery didn't replace mathematical judgment; it came after a year and a half of expert human work translating Scholze and Clausen's argument into a statement precise enough to formalize in the first place. The proof is airtight relative to that statement. Whether the statement is the theorem people care about was itself settled by ordinary mathematical community process, not by Lean.

A live, unresolved dispute makes the same point more starkly. Shinichi Mochizuki has claimed since 2012 to have proven the abc conjecture through what he calls Inter-Universal Teichmüller theory, and the papers were eventually published, in 2021, in a journal at the institute where he works—one he is also an editor of, a detail that sits squarely inside Rule 4's warning about institutional signals. In 2018, Peter Scholze (a Fields medalist) and Jakob Stix (a specialist in exactly this subfield) published a detailed report identifying what they describe as a specific, unresolved gap in the argument; [Quanta's coverage](https://www.quantamagazine.org/titans-of-mathematics-clash-over-epic-proof-of-abc-conjecture-20180920/) of the exchange is a good account of how sharply the two sides disagree. Mochizuki maintains their objection rests on a misreading of his framework. A third mathematician, Kirti Joshi, has since published an extended, still-evolving analysis arguing that the Scholze–Stix objection is itself mathematically mistaken, while separately arguing that Mochizuki's original proof needs additional work to be complete—a position neither of the other two camps has endorsed. As of the most recent material available, the broader mathematical community has not converged on accepting the proof. Whatever the eventual resolution, the entire dispute is, at its core, about whether Mochizuki's formal objects and definitions do what he says they do—a Rule 5 question—and no amount of pointing to the RIMS publication (Rule 4) or to the internal formal machinery of IUT (Rule 3) settles it on its own. It has to be settled the way Rule 5 says it has to be settled: by expert scrutiny of what the definitions actually mean, which is exactly the kind of scrutiny Rules 3 and 6 are at risk of treating as beneath a strong-enough formal claim.

**The practical fix**, before this becomes a skill: Rules 3, 6, and 7 should say explicitly that a formal or "Level 4" result is unconditionally established only *as formalized*. Rule 5's definitional audit should be a mandatory gate before a formal result is treated as having settled a real-world or domain-level dispute, not an optional closing check. Concretely: a challenge that targets whether the definitions map onto the intended claim doesn't need to be formalized to be a legitimate Level 4-defeating objection—it needs to be a serious argument that the formalization doesn't capture what it claims to. Only a challenge to the internal logical steps of an *already-agreed* formalization actually needs to meet the claim at Level 4. Skipping that distinction is how this framework could end up being used to declare victory by pointing at a Lean proof of a mis-specified theorem—which is exactly the failure mode Rule 5 exists to catch.

It's also worth separating two different domains this document treats as one. The replication-crisis evidence for Rule 3 (Ioannidis, the Open Science Collaboration) is about empirical, experimental science, where the dominant failure mode is a study that doesn't reproduce. Pure mathematics has a different failure mode—an undetected error in a deductive chain, or, as in the Mochizuki case, a disagreement about whether a formal framework does what it's claimed to do. The evidence for "peer review is a weak filter" transfers cleanly to the first domain and much less directly to the second. A skill built on this protocol should ask which kind of claim it's looking at before reaching for the replication-crisis citations.

## The test underneath all eight rules

Pull the threads together and there's a single question doing most of the actual work: strip away who is making the claim—the person, their community, their institution, the polish of the argument itself, Rule 1's job—and ask instead whether the claim can be reproduced, given the resources and the general direction of the question and its proposed answer.

That's Rule 3's bottom rung (empirical reproducibility), generalized to cover the other three instead of sitting below them on a ladder. A Lean proof is reproducible the moment anyone runs the same checker against the same statement—which is exactly why the internal-logic axis in Rule 3 collapses to near-zero effort once formalized. A psychological finding is reproducible, or isn't, in the direct sense the OSC project tested. Consilience is this same test run in its strongest form: not just "does it reproduce," but "does it reproduce again, by an unrelated method, that had no reason to agree unless the thing is real." The sharpest version of that test is a negative control—running the identical method on a case it has no business working on, the way the Bible Code rebuttal ran the same letter-skipping method on *Moby-Dick*. A pattern that "reproduces" there too was never about the claim; it was about the method. And neither consilience nor reproducibility means anything at all—the question is simply malformed—if the definitions aren't stated clearly enough for someone else to attempt it, which is Rule 5's condition for any of the rest of this mattering. On this view, credibility isn't a rung to climb; it's one question asked of the claim itself, independent of who's asking or answering: given what it would take to try this yourself, could you—and would it still work on something it shouldn't?

## Recommendations before this becomes a skill

- Rewrite Rule 3 as a domain-relative spectrum rather than one ladder: add consilience as the real, missing rung, and frame it as the empirical ceiling (what independent, unrelated convergent evidence can achieve for inductive claims) rather than as a fixed step below formal proof (the ceiling for deductive claims). Make explicit that demanding proof-of-the-mathematical-kind from an empirical field, or demanding cross-domain consilience for a narrow domain-specific finding, are both category errors, not calls for more rigor.
- Within that, keep the two-axis distinction: internal-logical assurance (genuinely hierarchical—formal proof collapses this to near-zero, peer review leaves much more of it undone) and definitional mapping (not moved by formalization, consilience, or reproduction alone—this is Rule 5's job, and it's where fabricated consilience, like the Bible Code affair or golden-ratio numerology, actually fails). Make clear that a Level 2-style objection aimed at the second axis isn't lower-tier just because it isn't formalized.
- Build in a concrete check for telling real consilience from a manufactured pattern: does it survive being run on a case with no reason to produce it (a negative control), and does the match involve shared relational/causal structure (Gentner) rather than a shared surface number chosen after the fact.
- Make that same definitional audit a required gate before Rules 3, 6, or 7 can be used to declare a dispute settled, not a separate, optional step at the end.
- Have the skill distinguish empirical/experimental claims (where the replication-crisis literature applies directly, and consilience is the achievable ceiling) from deductive/mathematical claims (where the real risk is a mis-specified formalization, not a failed replication).
- Rewrite the "use an LLM" instruction in Rules 2 and 7 to specify *which* use: checking a short passage of definitions/framing against the original, or driving search to find independently-checkable sources—both hold up well. Trusting a model's unverified output as the evidence itself is the failure mode to name and rule out, not "using LLMs" in general.
- Note that even "Level 4" formal proofs rest on residual assumptions (compiler, hardware, boot-code correctness, and, in principle, the prover's own soundness)—worth one line so the skill doesn't present machine-checked as a synonym for infallible.
- Consider building the closing test directly into the skill's instructions: before invoking Rule 3's hierarchy to settle something in your own favor, name the specific objection being overridden, confirm whether it targets internal logic or definitions, and ask whether the claim is reproducible given the stated resources. That keeps Rule 1's "zero epistemic skew" from quietly lapsing the moment a formal-looking result is on someone's own side.
